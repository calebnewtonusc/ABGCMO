# Activation and retention

**Acquisition before retention is a leaky bucket with a bigger hose.** If people
leave, more traffic makes the leak faster and the numbers look worse, not better.
This skill runs before any channel work.

## The number that decides where to spend

**60-70% of annual churn happens in the first 90 days.**

That single fact reorders everything. Onboarding is the retention lever. Win-back
campaigns, loyalty programs, and re-engagement emails are working on the 30-40%
that is left, which means most retention effort is aimed at the smaller half.

## Find the aha moment with data, not intuition

The aha moment is the specific action that predicts a user sticking. It is found
by comparing what retained users did in their first session against what churned
users did.

| Product | Aha moment |
|---|---|
| Slack | 2,000 team messages sent |
| Dropbox | First file in a shared folder |
| Notion | Second page created |
| Facebook | 7 friends in 10 days |

Note these are **specific and numeric**. "User understands the value" is not an
aha moment; it is a hope. If you cannot express it as a countable event, you have
not found it.

Companies that identify it properly report **2-3x activation improvement within
90 days**.

## Time to value is the control knob

| Finding | Value |
|---|---|
| Median time to value | ~36 h |
| Aha within 5 min vs. 15+ min | **+40% 30-day retention** |
| Teams measuring TTV instead of completion rate | +15-25% trial-to-paid |

Shorter TTV raises activation because fewer people abandon mid-flow. **Measure
time to value, not onboarding completion rate.** A user who finishes your tour and
gets nothing has completed onboarding and failed activation.

Worked example: a company cut time to first value from 8 minutes to 3 and saw
Day-1 retention move from 45% to 61%. Another cut session duration from 8 minutes
to 3 while activation *rose* 40% — the shorter flow was the better flow.

Dropbox cut signup to email and password only and lifted conversion **40%+**.

## Cohorts, not blended averages

A blended churn rate mixes your best customers from three years ago with the
wrong-fit customers you acquired last month. **It describes the average and
represents nobody.**

Group by signup month and track each independently. **Later cohorts should
outperform earlier ones.** If your July cohort retains better at month 6 than
January's did, your product, onboarding, and targeting are improving. If not,
you are acquiring worse-fit users, and that is an acquisition problem masquerading
as a retention problem.

Five views to start: monthly churn, voluntary vs. involuntary, first-90-day cohort
retention, failed-payment recovery, net revenue retention.

## Benchmarks

| Segment | Healthy monthly logo churn |
|---|---|
| Enterprise | <0.5% |
| Mid-market | 0.5-1.5% |
| SMB / prosumer | 2-4% |
| Consumer subscription | many lose 5-10% |

Best-in-class revenue churn is under 1%. NRR of 110-130% is top-performer
territory; SMB-focused median is 100-105%.

## Streaks and habit

Duolingo's streak is the cheapest retention mechanic that exists: the longer it
runs, the higher the psychological cost of breaking it. Combined with leagues and
reminders it produces genuine daily habit.

**But it only works if the daily action is genuinely small.** A streak on a
demanding action creates guilt and then abandonment. Duolingo also learned to
market *streak revival* — giving people a way back after breaking one, because
the alternative is losing them permanently.

## When retention cannot be fixed

Sometimes the honest answer is that there is no product-market fit and no
onboarding flow will manufacture it. Run the Sean Ellis test: **40% "very
disappointed"** is the threshold. Below it, segment down to the users who love it
most and find out what is different about them (Superhuman went 22% → 33% by
segmenting, then 58% by building for that segment). That is product work, not
growth work, and calling it correctly saves months.
