# Growth loops

Brian Balfour's reframe: **funnels are dead ends, loops compound.** A funnel is a
one-way path where you pour in traffic and some converts. A loop is a closed
system where the output feeds back into the input.

The practical question is not "how do I get more users" but **"what does a new
user do that produces the next user?"** If there is no answer, you do not have
growth, you have campaigns, and campaigns stop when you stop.

## The viral math

`K = i x c` — invites sent per user, times invite conversion rate.

| K | Meaning |
|---|---|
| > 1 | Self-sustaining. **Rare, and usually temporary.** Do not promise this. |
| = 1 | Linear |
| 0.3-0.7 | Where healthy products actually land, and still compounds usefully |

**Cycle time matters as much as K, and is usually easier to move.** A 3-day loop at
K=0.4 outgrows a 30-day loop at K=0.5. Before trying to raise K, ask how to
shorten the time between a user arriving and them producing the next one.

Levers on K: more invites sent (prompts, easy sharing), better invite conversion
(landing page, social proof), shorter cycle, product gets better with more users,
sharing built into the core workflow, double-sided rewards (reported up to 3x
better than single-sided).

## Loop types, and which one is yours

| Loop | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Collaborative** | Using it requires inviting someone | Figma, Slack, Notion. Highest K. |
| **Output** | The artifact carries the brand | Loom, Canva, Wordle, Spotify Wrapped |
| **Communication** | Both parties need it for the interaction | Zoom, Calendly. The link *is* the ad. |
| **Incentive** | Explicit reward for referring | Dropbox, Morning Brew |
| **Content/SEO** | Users generate pages that rank | Programmatic SEO, UGC platforms |
| **UGC** | Users make the content others come for | Roblox, Fortnite |
| **Ecosystem** | Third parties build on you and market you | Shopify apps, Claude Code skills |

**If none of these fit your product, it does not have a growth loop.** Say so.
Products without a loop grow through search, registries, and word of mouth, and
that is a legitimate outcome — but it is linear, and planning as if it compounds
will produce a bad plan.

## Designing the shareable artifact

Wordle is the reference implementation. The emoji grid worked because it:
- encoded the result **without spoiling** the experience for the recipient
- required no account, no app, no link — plain text that travels anywhere
- was **socially safe to post**, so sharing carried no cost
- functioned as a peer recommendation containing no ask

Most products bolt on a share button that broadcasts an ad for the company.
Wordle shipped a share format that broadcast *the person sharing it*. Design for
what makes the sharer look good, not what makes you look good.

## Nomination beats sharing

The ALS Ice Bucket Challenge raised $115M+ on one mechanic: **participants named
specific people in public.** A share is a broadcast anyone can ignore. A
nomination is a personal obligation with social visibility. The conversion rates
are not comparable.

## Reward design

- **Reward in product currency where possible.** Dropbox gave storage, not cash.
  It costs marginal COGS instead of margin.
- **Make progress visible.** Both Dropbox and Morning Brew put a live referral
  counter in front of the user. Invisible loops go quiet.
- **Physical rewards punch above their value.** Morning Brew's stickers cost ~$0.25
  per acquisition. People wanted the sticker because it proved membership.
- **Push the channels that actually convert.** Morning Brew found social and SMS
  referrals dwarfed email, and pushed those specifically.

## The four fits (Balfour)

Growth past ~$100M requires four things to line up, and a break in any one caps you:
**product-channel fit, channel-model fit, model-market fit, market-product fit.**
When a channel that worked stops working, the usual cause is that one of these
shifted, not that the tactics got stale.
