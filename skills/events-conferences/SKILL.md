# Events, conferences, and hackathons

## The honest economics

One good conversation at an event is worth roughly 50 cold emails [A]. That is the
case for going. The case against is that events are the easiest place to spend
$20k and feel productive while producing nothing.

**The value is in the hallway, not the stage.** Budget accordingly: a speaking
slot you use to run a demo is worth less than two days of scheduled coffees.

## Hackathon sponsorship

The open secret: **roughly 80% of the motivation is recruiting, 20% is brand
marketing** [A]. If you are not hiring, that 80% evaporates and you should
re-price the decision.

When it does work: ConsenSys sponsored a hackathon series reaching 500+ developers
across 8 cities, then ran its own event drawing 1,600+ participants, 70+ projects,
and 900+ Discord members [A]. Sponsoring first and running your own second is the
correct order — you learn the format on someone else's operational risk.

Prizes do not need to be large. Mercor sourced engineers at IIT hackathons with
prizes totalling roughly **$71**, viable because they could outbid on the dimension
that mattered locally. See `cases/marketplaces.md`.

## Speaking

The talk is not the point; the talk is the reason people will take a meeting.

- Submit six months out. CFPs close far earlier than founders expect.
- **A talk that teaches something specific gets accepted. A product talk does not.**
- The best-converting talk is the one where your product is the obvious answer to
  a problem you spent 25 minutes making the audience feel.
- Publish the talk as a written post the same day. The written version outlives the
  recording and is what gets linked.

## Running your own

Only when you have a community that already exists. An event is an amplifier, not
a generator. Notion gave ambassadors small budgets to run their own community
events — cheap, distributed, and credible because a peer convened it rather than
the company.

Small and repeated beats large and annual for a company under 50 people.

## Working an event properly

- [ ] **Book meetings before you go.** Walk-up traffic is the fallback, not the plan.
- [ ] Target 8-12 scheduled conversations per day
- [ ] Bring something people want that is not swag: a printed benchmark, a teardown,
      a checklist. It survives the flight home.
- [ ] Follow up within 48 hours referencing the specific thing you discussed
- [ ] Log every conversation the same night; you will not remember on Monday

## Measurement

Track **conversations that produced a follow-up**, not badge scans. Badge scans
measure foot traffic past a table. If an event produced fewer than five real
follow-ups, do not go back.

## Common failure

Buying a booth as a first event. Booths convert badly for unknown companies
because nobody is looking for you. Attend first, talk to people, and buy the
booth only once you know your buyer is in the room.
