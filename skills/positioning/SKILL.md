# Positioning

Positioning is context. The same product succeeds or fails on the frame people
understand it inside. This is the highest-leverage and most-skipped growth work.

**The proof case:** Notion in 2013 was "collaborative building apps, kind of like
document editing, and kind of like Xcode for the web," and nearly went bankrupt.
The 2016 relaunch described the **identical product** as a notes app and became a
category leader. Nothing about the ambition changed. See `cases/plg-saas.md`.

## April Dunford's process, in the order that matters

1. **List the competitive alternatives.** Not competitors — alternatives. Ask:
   *"what would this person do if we did not exist?"* In B2B roughly 40% of deals
   are lost to "no decision," which means you lost to a spreadsheet, to pen and
   paper, or to an intern. Those are your real alternatives.
2. **Isolate your unique attributes.** Features the alternatives genuinely lack.
   Start with feature differences, then translate each into delivered value.
3. **Find the value that only you deliver**, and that someone actually cares about.
4. **Identify who cares a lot.** The segment for whom that value is urgent. Be
   specific enough that it feels uncomfortably narrow.
5. **Choose the market category** that makes the value obvious. Category is the
   context that triggers a buyer's assumptions about competitors, pricing, and
   expected features. Choosing well means you barely have to explain yourself.

## Category: enter one or create one

**Enter an existing category** when the category is understood and you can be
obviously best for a specific segment. Faster, cheaper, right far more often than
founders want. Notion did this deliberately.

**Create a category** only when no existing frame fits and being compared inside
one would actively misrepresent you. It is expensive: you are paying to educate a
market. The playbook (HubSpot, Drift) is: **name it, own the definition, build the
media around it** — manifesto, book, podcast, conference. See
`cases/category-creation.md`.

## The strategic narrative (Andy Raskin)

For a pitch, a deck, or a launch post, the structure that works:

1. **Name a shift in the world** — an undeniable change that is already happening.
2. **Show winners and losers** — who is thriving because of the shift, who is not.
3. **Tease the promised land** — the desirable future state, described as a state
   of being rather than as your product.
4. **Show your capabilities as "magic gifts"** for reaching it.
5. **Present evidence** you can deliver.

Note what this does not do: lead with the product. The product enters at step 4.

## Diagnostics

- **The five-second test.** Can someone say what category this is in, in five
  seconds? If not, conversion collapses before any other factor applies.
- **The one-line test.** Notion's word of mouth was *"I cancelled Evernote,
  Trello, and our wiki."* Concrete, comparative, repeatable by a non-expert. If
  your users cannot repeat your pitch, there is no word of mouth.
- **The portability test.** If your description would fit a competitor unchanged,
  it is not positioning, it is category boilerplate.

## Crowded-category rule

When the category is crowded, do not argue you are better. **Find the true
sub-category where you are obviously first**, and lead with the attribute nobody
else has. "My Claude Code kit" competes with hundreds; "the one that drives your
Mac" competes with almost none.
