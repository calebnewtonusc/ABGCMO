# The cold start problem

Andrew Chen's framing: network effects are **destructive** at inception. Early
users churn *because* nobody else is there. You cannot grow your way out; you have
to assemble one working network first.

## Atomic network

The **atomic network** is the smallest group that makes the product self-sustaining.
Build one and you can usually build many, because each seeds the next.

Getting this right means **deliberately constraining scope**. Facebook stayed
inside Harvard until saturation, then the Ivies, then all colleges. Graham's
image: keep the fire contained until it is really hot before adding logs.

Ask: what is the smallest unit — one campus, one Discord, one company, one city,
one subreddit — where this becomes useful with everyone in it participating?

## The hard side

One side is harder to acquire, and the other follows it. Name it explicitly:

| Network | Hard side |
|---|---|
| Marketplace | Sellers / supply |
| Content platform | Creators |
| Rideshare | Drivers |
| Dev tool ecosystem | Contributors |
| Dating app | Whichever side is scarcer locally |
| Community | The people who post, not the people who read |

Then spend nearly all effort there. Uber, Airbnb, and Tinder all did supply first.

## Do things that don't scale

The specific moves, from Paul Graham's 2013 essay and the case ledger:

- **Recruit manually.** Almost every startup has to. Waiting for users is how
  projects die quietly.
- **The Collison installation.** Stripe's founders did not ask "will you try our
  beta?" They said **"give me your laptop"** and set it up on the spot.
- **Do the work the user won't.** Airbnb photographed apartments themselves
  because listings with real photos converted and hosts would not do it.
- **Go to where the network already meets.** Tinder ran sorority-house
  presentations and walked to the fraternity so the payoff was visible in the
  room. 5,000 to 15,000 users on that tour alone.
- **Overserve the first cohort.** Graham says he has never seen a startup harmed
  by trying too hard to make early users happy. Notion's founders replied to
  support in about a minute.
- **Start with your actual network.** Mercor's first clients were classmates.

## Manufacture the moment

Airbnb built supply in specific cities **during events when hotels sold out** —
concentrating where the alternative had failed. Look for the moment your
category's default option breaks, and be there.

## Warm beats cold by an order of magnitude

Warm introductions convert at 30-50%. Cold outreach converts at 2-5%. Your
existing network almost certainly contains 5-10 potential users or people who can
introduce you. Exhaust that completely before buying strangers.

## Exclusivity is free

You can gate access before you can justify it. Superhuman hand-picked founders and
VCs off the waitlist, seeding exactly the networks they wanted. Mercor made
*companies* apply so the talent felt premium while matching was still manual
Google Sheets.

The counterweight: Figma ran a closed beta and **later said they regretted it**,
because it slowed feedback velocity. Gate when you need positioning. Do not gate
when you need learning.
