# Category creation

> "If you define the category, you set the buying criteria, and you are, by
> definition, the category leader on day one."

---

## HubSpot — "inbound marketing"

Rather than entering the crowded CRM or email space with a marginally better
feature set, HubSpot staked out new conceptual territory: **inbound marketing** as
a philosophy, a methodology, and eventually a culture. The moat was **intellectual
ownership of a category**, delivered through content and community. Competitors
had to argue against a worldview, not a feature list.

---

## Drift — "conversational marketing"

Explicitly modeled on HubSpot's playbook. David Cancel and Dave Gerhardt launched
Drift in 2015. The move came in 2016: they **removed every lead-capture form from
their own website** and declared a new category. Then they executed the full
category-creation kit —
- a manifesto,
- a podcast,
- the book *Conversational Marketing*,
- the HYPERGROWTH conference.

**The playbook, stated plainly: name it, own the definition, build the media
around it.** Removing their own forms was the proof-of-conviction move; it made
the category claim credible and newsworthy at the same time.

---

## When category creation is wrong

It is expensive and slow. You are paying to educate a market. Choose it only when
your product genuinely does not fit an existing category and being compared
inside one would make you look worse.

The alternative — **positioning inside an existing category as obviously best for
a specific segment** — is faster and cheaper, and it is the right call far more
often than founders want it to be. See `skills/positioning`.

Notion is the counter-example that proves it: they did **not** create a category.
They deliberately entered the well-understood "notes app" category, because that
context made an unfamiliar product legible. The ambition stayed the same; the
category label was chosen for comprehension.
