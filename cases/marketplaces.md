# Marketplaces and two-sided networks

The governing theory is Andrew Chen's *Cold Start Problem*. Network effects are
**destructive** at inception: early users churn precisely because nobody else is
there. The job is not growth, it is assembling one **atomic network** — the
smallest group that makes the product self-sustaining. Build one and you can
usually build many, because each seeds the next.

Inside every network there is a **hard side**: the participants who are harder
to acquire and who the other side follows. Naming the hard side reorders
everything.

---

## Uber

**Mechanism:** perfect one city, document the playbook, replicate.

Uber solved San Francisco first and wrote down a replicable launch playbook
before expanding city by city. The hard side was drivers: riders follow supply,
supply does not follow demand. Every city launch was a fresh cold start,
which is why the playbook mattered more than the product.

**Transferable:** if your network is geographically or vertically segmented,
you do not have one cold start problem. You have N of them. Solve one
completely and write down how.

---

## Airbnb

**Mechanism:** categorical density plus doing things that don't scale.

Rather than spreading thin, Airbnb built deep supply in specific cities during
events where hotel rooms were scarce — manufacturing a moment when the
alternative failed. The founders personally photographed apartments in New York,
because listings with real photos converted and hosts would not take the photos
themselves.

**Transferable:** find the moment when the incumbent alternative is unavailable
or painful, and concentrate there. And if a quality bar is blocking liquidity,
you do that work by hand until it is automatable.

---

## DoorDash

**Mechanism:** compete where the incumbents are not.

DoorDash deliberately launched in **suburbs and mid-sized cities** rather than
fighting Uber and Grubhub in dense urban cores. They built overwhelming density
in an underserved vertical first, then took the competitive markets with the
operational learnings and the balance sheet from the easy ones. The founders
personally delivered food in the early days.

**Transferable:** the obvious market is obvious to your competitors too. Winning
a market nobody is contesting is worth more than losing one everybody wants.

---

## Tinder

**Mechanism:** solve the hard side in person, one house at a time.

Whitney Wolfe, then Tinder's marketing lead and a Kappa Kappa Gamma member at
SMU, ran presentations at sorority chapters and had every woman in the room
install the app. Then she walked to the corresponding fraternity, where the men
opened the app and saw women they already knew. Women were the hard side; the
demo was the proof.

That campus tour took Tinder from **5,000 to 15,000 users**. They then scaled the
unscalable part: parties where the price of admission was showing the app on your
phone, replicated through campus reps nationally.

**Transferable:** the hard side is not acquired with ads. It is acquired in
person, by someone with standing in that community, with the payoff visible in
the room. This is the single most copyable case in the ledger for a student.

---

## Mercor

**Mechanism:** classmates first, then compensation arbitrage, then manual matching.

Started as a dev agency in January 2022. First clients were Brendan Foody's
classmates at Georgetown. To source engineers they sponsored hackathons at the
IITs with prizes totalling roughly **$71** — viable because big-tech offers in
India paid the equivalent of ~$5/hour, so they could outbid on the dimension
that mattered locally. They also ran Facebook ads for candidates, which almost
nobody does.

The pivot to a talent marketplace came from **customer pull**: clients kept
asking to hire the engineers directly. Matching was manual — resumes reviewed by
the founders, connections made over WhatsApp, contracts through PandaDoc, tracking
in Google Sheets — until the process broke, at which point they automated exactly
the part that had broken.

They also made **companies apply**, gatekeeping access so the talent felt premium
before the backend justified it. Nearly every early customer came through Prod, a
student-run founder community.

Now roughly a **$2B revenue run rate**.

**Transferable:** four things. Start with the network you actually have. Recruit
where you have an unfair advantage rather than where everyone competes. Do it by
hand until it breaks, because that tells you what to automate. And exclusivity is
free — you can gate access before you can justify it.
